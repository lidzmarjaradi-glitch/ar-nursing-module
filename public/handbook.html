Chapter 1

Cardiovascular System – The Heart

Anatomy Overview

The heart is a muscular organ approximately the size of a closed fist, located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs. It functions as the central pump of the cardiovascular system, circulating blood throughout the body to deliver oxygen and nutrients while removing metabolic waste products.

The heart consists of four chambers: two upper chambers called atria that receive blood, and two lower chambers called ventricles that pump blood out of the heart. The right side of the heart handles deoxygenated blood from the body, while the left side manages oxygenated blood from the lungs.

🩺 Clinical Relevance

Understanding cardiac anatomy is essential for proper auscultation of heart sounds. The four main auscultation points correspond to the closure of the four heart valves: aortic (right 2nd intercostal space), pulmonic (left 2nd intercostal space), tricuspid (left lower sternal border), and mitral (5th intercostal space, midclavicular line).

Clinical Assessment

When assessing cardiovascular function, nurses should evaluate:

  • Heart rate and rhythm through pulse palpation
  • Blood pressure measurements
  • Heart sounds via auscultation
  • Peripheral circulation including capillary refill
  • Signs of cardiac distress or failure

Chapter 2

Respiratory System – The Lungs

Anatomy Overview

The lungs are paired organs located in the thoracic cavity, protected by the rib cage. The right lung has three lobes (superior, middle, and inferior), while the left lung has two lobes (superior and inferior) with a cardiac notch to accommodate the heart.

Gas exchange occurs at the alveoli, tiny air sacs at the terminal ends of the bronchial tree. Each lung contains approximately 300 million alveoli, providing a surface area of about 70 square meters for efficient oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange.

🩺 Clinical Relevance

Respiratory assessment includes auscultation of breath sounds at multiple locations. Normal breath sounds include vesicular (peripheral lung), bronchial (over trachea), and bronchovesicular (near sternum). Abnormal sounds such as crackles, wheezes, or stridor indicate pathology requiring further evaluation.

⚠️ Clinical Alert

Decreased breath sounds on one side may indicate pneumothorax, pleural effusion, or atelectasis. Immediate assessment and intervention may be required.

Chapter 3

Urinary System – The Kidney

Anatomy Overview

The kidneys are bean-shaped organs located retroperitoneally on either side of the spine. Each kidney is approximately 11 cm long and weighs about 150 grams. They filter blood to produce urine, regulate electrolyte balance, and maintain acid-base homeostasis.

Each kidney contains approximately one million nephrons, the functional units responsible for filtration. Blood enters through the renal artery and exits through the renal vein, while urine drains via the ureter to the bladder.

🩺 Clinical Relevance

Kidney assessment includes monitoring urine output (normal: 0.5-1 mL/kg/hr), evaluating laboratory values (BUN, creatinine, GFR), and physical examination for costovertebral angle tenderness. Understanding kidney anatomy aids in recognizing signs of renal dysfunction.

Chapter 4

Nervous System – The Brain

Anatomy Overview

The brain is the control center of the nervous system, weighing approximately 1.4 kg in adults. It is protected by the skull, meninges, and cerebrospinal fluid. The brain regulates all body functions, processes sensory information, and enables cognition, emotion, and behavior.

The cerebrum, the largest part of the brain, is divided into two hemispheres connected by the corpus callosum. Each hemisphere contains four lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital, each with specialized functions.

🩺 Clinical Relevance

Neurological assessment includes evaluating level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale), pupillary response, motor function, and cranial nerve function. Understanding brain anatomy helps localize lesions and predict deficits associated with stroke or trauma.

⚠️ Clinical Alert

Signs of increased intracranial pressure (headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, papilledema) require immediate medical attention as they may indicate life-threatening conditions.

Clinical Procedures – Injection Technique

Equipment Overview

The medical syringe is a fundamental tool for medication administration. Understanding its components ensures safe and effective injection technique. Syringes come in various sizes (1mL, 3mL, 5mL, 10mL) and are typically disposable to prevent cross-contamination.

Proper technique includes maintaining sterile principles, selecting appropriate needle gauge and length for the injection route, and understanding anatomical landmarks for safe administration sites.

🩺 Clinical Relevance

Injection routes include intradermal (ID), subcutaneous (SC), intramuscular (IM), and intravenous (IV). Each route requires specific needle selection and technique. IM injections commonly use the deltoid, ventrogluteal, or vastus lateralis sites.

Safe Injection Practices

  • Verify the "5 Rights": Right patient, drug, dose, route, and time
  • Perform hand hygiene and use aseptic technique
  • Aspirate before IM injections (except vaccines per current guidelines)
  • Dispose of sharps immediately in designated containers
  • Document the procedure and monitor for adverse reactions
